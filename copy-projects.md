# A project that already knows your work, and shows where it learned it

## Several product teams were each solving "projects" from inside their own app, so a small neutral team was asked to start from the work itself. I made a workspace that organizes itself from your real work, catches you up, and acts for you, with a receipt on everything it claims to know.

A matter, a launch, a deal runs for months, and every time you ask an assistant for help you rebuild the context by hand. Every product team saw this through its own lens. Content here, chat there. Nobody owned the project itself. In July a handful of us were pulled together because we were neutral, and told to start from the unit of work instead of the app. We made a repo the same day.

Home opens on cards made from your own work: requests to you, commitments you made, decisions waiting on you. Each says why it is there and links to where it came from. Beneath them, suggested projects name the workstreams already running through your files, chats, and meetings. Nothing was set up.

Accept a suggestion, answer two questions, press Create. The project fills in with the real files, chats, and meetings behind it, each linked where it lives rather than copied, each moving from preparing to ready. That was the first boundary I held. A reference is a pointer, not a copy, and not proof the system has read it.

Come back later and the project writes what changed since your last visit, with a citation on every point. What it cannot verify it holds back, and says so. In September I split the prototype in two, what people see on top and a data layer underneath that pulls commitments and decisions out of real sources with a receipt on each, and the catch-up is that layer showing its work. A summary that is right most of the time teaches you to stop reading it. One that tells you what it does not know is one you can act on.

Acting runs the same way. Ask for an email or a meeting from the project's evidence and it drafts one for you to confirm. Say "make it shorter" and the same draft changes in place, with a note on what changed.

Sharing is where I argued the value was. Products collapse three questions into one Share button: who is in the project, what belongs in shared context, and who can open the original file. The model keeps them apart. Adding someone publishes the project's name and goal and nothing else; every document, note, and conversation is a separate, deliberate move, and the original never changes who can open it. Each person keeps a private space inside the shared project. At the moment of commitment the dialog says what changes and what does not, in plain words. The first step is live against real storage; the rest is made and tested offline, its live rehearsal still ahead.

The team stayed small and the loops short: one-day points of view, parallel branches, a competing front end retired rather than maintained. My work is the boundary model, the catch-up, the act-and-revise contract, and the share model; the data pipelines and the component system already existed. The seams are plain. Making a shared project private again is an open question, and cost is not yet legible to the person paying for it. I would rather show those than paper over them.
