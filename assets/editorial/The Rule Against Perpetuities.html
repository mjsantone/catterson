Property Law 1682 – Present

The Rule
Against Perpetuities

Doctrine A deep-research brief · 18 min read

One sentence of law has confused every generation of lawyers for three centuries — so reliably that a state supreme court once ruled it is not malpractice to get it wrong. Now that same difficulty is quietly killing the rule off.

The Gist In short
  • The rule is a single sentence — and a trap. "No interest is good unless it must vest, if at all, not later than 21 years after some life in being."
  • Courts excused the failure. In Lucas v. Hamm (1961), California held that a will violating the rule — costing heirs $75,000 — was not attorney negligence.
  • It is being dismantled. Since South Dakota abolished it in 1983, states have raced to authorize trusts that last centuries — or forever.
1
The rule, briefly

Twenty-six words that hide a monster.

The Rule Against Perpetuities is, on its face, one of the most economical statements in Anglo-American law. Set down most famously by the property scholar John Chipman Gray and paraphrased by W. Barton Leach in his 1938 essay Perpetuities in a Nutshell, it reads: "No interest is good unless it must vest, if at all, not later than 21 years after some life in being at the creation of the interest." Twenty-six words. A single sentence. And yet generations of law students, practicing attorneys, and even judges have found it nearly impossible to apply without error.

What the rule tries to do is simple enough to state in plain English. It prevents a person from using a deed or a will to control the ownership of property for an unreasonably long time after their death — from reaching out from the grave, in effect, to bind land and wealth to conditions that could persist for centuries. As the Cornell Legal Information Institute puts it, if an interest in real property does not vest within "21 years of life-in-being" at the creation of the interest, then that interest "is not good." The underlying policy is old and coherent: the common law disapproves of restraints that remove property from the marketplace and freeze its ownership across unknowable spans of future time.

The trouble is that the sentence's brevity is a disguise. Every operative word — interest, vest, life in being, must — carries a technical, often counterintuitive meaning, and the rule demands that the validity of a gift be judged not by what actually happens but by every hypothetical thing that could conceivably happen, no matter how absurd. It is this move from the actual to the merely possible that transforms a tidy policy into what one court would eventually call a "technicality-ridden legal nightmare."

What the rule actually touches

Critically, the rule does not apply to the ordinary interests most people imagine when they think of property — a fee simple or a life estate, which normally vest immediately. It applies only to a narrow band of future interests. As legal educators at Quimbee and Thomson Reuters summarize, these are chiefly three: contingent remainders; executory interests (which include options to purchase and rights of first refusal); and vested remainders subject to open — typically class gifts that are vested for some members of a class but still open to new ones. Powers of appointment round out the list.

Because the target is so specific, the rule is easy to overlook and easy to trip over in the same instrument. A drafter can spend a career without seeing it, then bury a fatal violation in a single clause of a residuary trust — which is very nearly what happened in the case that made the rule notorious. But before that case, it is worth understanding why the sentence is so treacherous, because the answer explains both the courtroom sympathy the doctrine attracts and the legislative impatience that is now dismantling it.

2
Why it breaks minds

The fertile octogenarian and other ghosts.

The rule's difficulty is not a matter of arithmetic; it is a matter of imagination. To test whether a gift is valid, the analyst must ask not "will this vest in time?" but "is there any possibility — however remote, however biologically preposterous — that it could vest too late?" A single imaginable scenario in which the interest vests more than twenty-one years after every relevant life in being has ended is enough to void the gift entirely, at the moment of its creation, before the world has had a chance to prove the fear groundless.

This is what generates the doctrine's famous menagerie of absurd hypotheticals. The most celebrated is the fertile octogenarian. Because the common law conclusively presumes that any living person, of any age, is capable of having more children, a gift conditioned on the children of an eighty-year-old woman can fail — the law insists on entertaining the possibility of a future child who could then satisfy the condition decades beyond the measuring lives. Its companion is the unborn widow: a gift to the widow of a living man can fail because the law allows that his eventual widow might be a woman not yet born at the time the interest is created, blowing past the permissible period.

Practitioners have developed rough survival tactics for exactly this reason. A widely shared piece of folk wisdom among law students — captured vividly in online study forums — is to "start by killing off the lives in being": imagine every relevant person dies tomorrow, then map how long it would take for the interest to vest. If it must vest at their deaths, there is usually no problem. If it could vest more than twenty-one years afterward, there is almost certainly a violation. Another common heuristic: if a fact pattern reaches a beneficiary more than two generations below the grantor, suspect a perpetuities problem — but "identify the issue, don't worry about the math," because the math is where careers go to die.

The candor of practicing lawyers on this point is remarkable. It is not unusual to hear an accomplished attorney say, flatly, that they never understood the rule and have made peace with never understanding it. This is not laziness; it is the predictable result of a doctrine that asks the human mind to reason exhaustively about improbable futures while treating the merely conceivable as if it were certain. Which raises an obvious and uncomfortable question: if the experts cannot reliably get it right, what happens when one of them gets it wrong — and a family loses money because of it?

3
The case that excused the lawyers

Lucas v. Hamm: when the court forgave the mistake.

The answer came from the Supreme Court of California in 1961, in a case that has since become the doctrine's signature anecdote. In Lucas v. Hamm (56 Cal.2d 583, 364 P.2d 685), the beneficiaries of Eugene H. Emmick's will — led by Robert Lucas — sued the attorney L.S. Hamm, who had drafted the will. A residual trust provision Hamm wrote was declared invalid, in part because it ran afoul of the rule against perpetuities and the related rule against unreasonable restraints on alienation. The trust was set to terminate five years after the probate court's order distributing property to the trustee — a clause that could, in a remote scenario, cause vesting to fall outside a life in being plus the permissible period. Forced to settle with the testator's blood relatives, the intended beneficiaries received roughly seventy-five thousand dollars less than the will had directed.

The case matters for two reasons, and they pull in opposite directions. On the doctrine of legal malpractice, Lucas is a landmark of expansion: overruling the older privity requirement of Buckley v. Gray, the court held that intended beneficiaries — though not the attorney's clients — could sue a drafting lawyer for negligence when their harm was foreseeable. That holding opened the door to third-party liability for negligent will drafting across American law. But on the specific claim before it, the court walked back through that very door and shut it. It found that Hamm was not negligent, because the rule he had violated was so forbidding that even a competent lawyer could reasonably fail to master it.

The language the court used to justify letting Hamm off the hook has become the doctrine's epitaph, quoted in law-review footnotes and study guides ever since. The court described the rule against perpetuities and its companion restraints as a "technicality-ridden legal nightmare" and "a dangerous instrumentality in the hands of most members of the bar." In the opinion itself, the court observed that "few, if any, [areas] of the law have been fraught with more confusion or concealed more traps for the unwary draftsman." Applying the ordinary standard of "skill, prudence, and diligence" that lawyers commonly exercise, it concluded it "would not be proper" to hold that Hamm had fallen short.

It is difficult to overstate how unusual this reasoning is. The professional standard of care asks whether a practitioner exercised the competence ordinary members of the profession command. When a court finds that ordinary members of the profession cannot reliably command a rule, it is not merely excusing one lawyer — it is issuing a public verdict on the rule itself. Lucas v. Hamm is, in that sense, less a defense of L.S. Hamm than an indictment of the doctrine he was asked to navigate. And once a legal system has conceded that its own rule is beyond the reliable grasp of its own experts, the pressure to reform or abandon that rule becomes almost irresistible.

4
The reform wave

Wait-and-see, cy-près, and a flat 90 years.

Reform came in layers, each trying to defuse a different part of the trap. The most consequential move was to abandon the "what could conceivably happen" test in favor of a "what actually happened" test — the wait-and-see doctrine, sometimes called the "second-look" doctrine. Instead of judging a suspect interest's validity at the moment of creation on the basis of every imaginable future, wait-and-see waits: it looks at the facts as they actually unfold, and only invalidates an interest if it truly fails to vest within the permitted period. This single change disarms the fertile octogenarian and the unborn widow, because those ghosts almost never materialize in reality.

A second mechanism, the cy-près (or reformation) approach, lets a court rewrite an offending grant to bring it within the rule rather than striking it down entirely — for example by reducing an offensive age contingency to twenty-one years. California's Civil Code, as the state's Law Revision Commission recorded, allowed reformation of interests invalid under the common-law rule through a court proceeding at any time after creation. These softer tools spread through the states, but they remained tied to the notoriously slippery concept of "measuring lives."

The decisive simplification arrived with the Uniform Statutory Rule Against Perpetuities (USRAP), promulgated by the Uniform Law Commission in 1986 after the American Law Institute's 1979 Restatement (Second) formally embraced wait-and-see. USRAP's key innovation was to replace "lives in being plus twenty-one years" with a single, easy-to-count number. Its operative language: an interest that is not certain to vest within the period of the common-law rule is nonetheless valid if it actually vests within ninety years of its creation. No measuring lives to identify, no biology to worry about — just a flat clock.

By most counts, USRAP was widely embraced. Wikipedia lists thirty-one adopting jurisdictions as of 2018 — including Alabama, Alaska, Arizona, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Florida, Georgia, Hawaii, Indiana, Kansas, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Nevada, New Jersey, North Carolina, Oregon, South Carolina, South Dakota, Tennessee, Utah, Virginia, and Washington, plus the District of Columbia and the U.S. Virgin Islands. Legal commentators counted twenty-three USRAP states as early as 1995. Yet even as the ninety-year fix spread, a very different and more radical movement was gathering — one that would treat the ninety-year backstop not as a simplification to be adopted but as a limit to be escaped.

5
The abolition era

The dynasty trust and the death of the rule.

In 1983, South Dakota did something no state had done: it abolished the rule against perpetuities outright. The relevant statute, SDCL §43-5-8, states plainly that "the common-law rule against perpetuities is not in force in this state." As the trust firm Bridgeford Trust and the law firm Robins Kaplan both recount, South Dakota was the first state in the nation to take this step — and in doing so it cleared the way for the modern dynasty trust, a vehicle that can last in perpetuity, forever, never triggering the forced distribution that would expose its assets to federal estate taxation.

The economic engine behind this shift is the federal Generation-Skipping Transfer Tax and its exemption. A trust that never terminates can, in principle, shelter wealth from transfer taxation across an unlimited number of generations by leveraging that exemption once and then compounding within the trust indefinitely. As the Nolan Law Firm's state survey explains, this created a scramble for "trust situs" — states competing to become the preferred legal home for the world's dynastic wealth by offering the most permissive perpetuities regime, alongside favorable tax, privacy, and asset-protection laws.

Other states followed with a spectrum of approaches. Some abolished the rule as South Dakota had — Wikipedia lists Alaska, Idaho, New Jersey, Kentucky, Rhode Island, and South Dakota among the outright repealers. Others kept a rule on the books but extended the permissible duration to figures so large as to be functionally perpetual. According to the Nolan survey and industry commentary, Nevada permits trusts to last 365 years, Tennessee 360 years, and Wyoming 1,000 years; some jurisdictions have reached for terms of 500 or even 1,000 years. Delaware operates a hybrid: it abolished the rule for personal property held in trust — enabling perpetual dynasty trusts built on intangible assets like LLC and corporate interests — while capping trusts holding real property at 110 years.

The power-of-sale workaround

A recurring technical device unites the abolitionist states. Because the deeper policy behind perpetuities has always been keeping property alienable — sellable, transferable, in the marketplace — many states condition the escape from the rule on the trustee retaining the power to sell the trust assets. Missouri's statute, for instance, provides that the rule does not apply to a trust if the trustee holds the power to sell the property during any period the trust continues beyond the common-law perpetuities window. South Dakota and Idaho similarly condition their repeals on the absence of a suspension of the power of alienation, typically satisfied by granting the trustee a power of sale. The logic is elegant: the trust may last forever, but the property inside it never becomes frozen, so the original economic evil the rule guarded against is technically avoided.

The states declined to reform not only their perpetuities rules but their transparency regimes to match. South Dakota, for example, requires courts to seal trust filings on request and protects those documents in perpetuity — a level of secrecy that, as Robins Kaplan notes, distinguishes it even from Delaware, which permits sealing for only three years. The combined package — no perpetuities limit, no state income tax on properly sitused trust assets, and permanent confidentiality — is why South Dakota has ranked, year after year, at or near the top of the industry's dynasty-trust rankings.

The map today

A fractured jurisdictional landscape.

The result is that the phrase "the rule against perpetuities" no longer describes a single American doctrine. It describes a patchwork. In one state a trust must wind up within a life in being plus twenty-one years; cross a border and it may run for centuries; cross another and it may run forever. A handful of states have deliberately stayed on the traditional side of the line. New York, notably, declined to abolish its rule — a choice that trust-industry whitepapers frame, unsurprisingly self-servingly, as an "outdated approach" that "restricts asset preservation." Louisiana and Rhode Island impose comparatively short limits. The persistence of these holdouts means the doctrine is neither dead nor alive nationally; it is alive in some places, embalmed in others, and buried in the rest.

7
What it means

A four-century rule, undone by its own difficulty.

There is an irony at the center of this story worth naming plainly. The rule against perpetuities was designed to prevent exactly the outcome that its own dismantling now enables: the indefinite concentration of wealth and control of property across unlimited generations. For three hundred years it stood, however clumsily, as a brake on dynastic accumulation. Its collapse is not the result of a considered policy judgment that dynastic wealth is good; it is, in significant part, the byproduct of the rule being too hard to live with — and of state legislatures discovering that they could profit handsomely by being the ones to abandon it.

The Lucas v. Hamm concession — that the rule defeats even competent lawyers — was an early admission of a doctrine in trouble. It is a short conceptual distance from "our experts cannot reliably apply this rule" to "let us replace it with a flat ninety-year clock," and a shorter distance still to "let us abolish it and capture the trust business." The intellectual difficulty that generated courtroom sympathy in 1961 became, by the 1980s and after, a competitive opening. Difficulty did not merely embarrass the rule; it helped kill it.

For anyone drafting or advising today, the practical takeaways are concrete. First, situs is now a first-order decision, not an afterthought: where a trust is created can determine whether it lasts twenty-one years past a life or forever. Second, the classic saving clause — a provision that forces a trust to terminate within the perpetuities period no matter what — remains prudent in any jurisdiction that retains a rule, and some commentators predicted it would become standard boilerplate. Third, the tax logic that drives perpetual trusts is federal, but the durational logic is state, and the two must be reasoned about together. The dynasty trust is not a loophole so much as the natural product of a federal exemption meeting fifty divergent state answers to a once-uniform question.

8
Scope & open questions

What this brief did and did not settle.

This report is a synthesis of publicly available legal explanation, the reported text of Lucas v. Hamm, and secondary summaries of state statutes drawn from law-firm surveys, trust-industry publications, and encyclopedic sources. It is not legal advice, and several of its figures deserve caveats. State durational limits are moving targets — legislatures amend them frequently, and different surveys report different numbers for the same state (some sources, for instance, place New York's dynasty-trust limit at 75 years while others emphasize that New York retained a traditional rule and did not create a dynasty-trust regime at all). The bar chart above should be read as illustrative of the range, not as a precise, currently-authoritative table for any specific transaction.

Several genuinely open questions remain beyond the scope of a brief like this one. Whether the mass shift to perpetual trusts will provoke a federal legislative response — for example, a limit on the generation-skipping exemption's duration — is unresolved and politically contingent. Whether the constitutional treatment of trust income (touched by the Supreme Court's Kaestner decision on state taxation of undistributed trust income) will further entrench no-tax situs states is an evolving area. And the deepest normative question — whether a legal system should permit the dead to bind property forever, and what that means for economic mobility over centuries — is precisely the question the old rule answered, and the one the new regime has quietly reopened without much public debate.

What is beyond dispute is the shape of the arc. A rule so difficult that courts excused lawyers for failing it has, within a single professional lifetime, gone from near-universal common-law doctrine to a fractured and largely optional constraint — reformed into a flat clock in some states, extended into near-eternity in others, and formally erased in the states that decided there was money in erasing it.
