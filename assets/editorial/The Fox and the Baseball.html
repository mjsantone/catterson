First possession

At what moment does a wild thing become yours?

A scroll story in two cases

The Fox and the Baseball

Pierson v. Post, 3 Cai. R. 175 (N.Y. Sup. Ct. 1805) Popov v. Hayashi, 2002 WL 31833731 (Cal. Super. Ct. Dec. 18, 2002)
An empty Atlantic dune beach at first light, wind in the grass
Act I — The Beach

An unowned animal, on unowned ground.

Southampton, New York. December 10, 1802.
The chase

Lodowick Post rode out with his hounds. They started a fox and ran it across open ground — in the words of the case record, "wild and uninhabited, unpossessed and waste land, called the beach." The fox belonged to no one. That was the whole point.

The intercept

As the hounds closed, Jesse Pierson killed the fox and carried it off. Post sued. A local justice ruled that the chase was enough — the fox was Post's. Pierson appealed to the New York Supreme Court of Judicature.

Where the record is silent

Whether Pierson saw the hunt bearing down behind the fox was never settled. He denied it. The court decided the case as if it did not matter.

The rule of capture, 1805

Pursuit is not possession.

The court reversed. To own a wild animal you must capture it — mortal wounding, or seizure in hand. The chase, however long and however close, earns nothing at law.

“Mere pursuit gave Post no legal right to the fox, but… he became the property of Pierson, who intercepted and killed him.

— Tompkins, J., for the majority, Pierson v. Post (1805)
The authorities

There was no settled precedent for a fox on a beach. To draw the line, the court reached far back:

Justinian's Institutes6th century
Bracton & Fleta13th century
Pufendorf17th century

From these it synthesized a single principle: occupancy — actual capture — makes property in a wild animal.

The dissent

Justice Livingston would have decided it differently. Put the question to sportsmen, he suggested, and give the fox to the hunter with a real prospect of taking it. The almost-catch, to him, deserved something. To him, Pierson was —

“a saucy intruder, who had not shared in the honours or labours of the chase.

— Livingston, J., dissenting, Pierson v. Post (1805)

He lost. The almost counted for nothing.

The question waits

One hundred and ninety-six years. Open beach to city ballpark; daylight to floodlight. The rule holds still.

Anonymous hands reaching upward in a dense night crowd under floodlight
Act II — The Arcade

One ball, coming down into a crowd.

San Francisco. October 7, 2001 — the last game of the season.
The catch

Barry Bonds hit his seventy-third home run of the year — a record. The ball came down into a standing-room walkway behind right field. Alex Popov got his glove up, and the ball went into the webbing.

The pile

Then the crowd came down on him. In the pile, the ball came loose. Patrick Hayashi — knocked to the ground himself, and found by the court to have done nothing wrong — saw it on the concrete and picked it up.

A single scuffed baseball resting on bare concrete under one hard light
Exhibit

One ball. Two claims.

A fox with stitches, loose on open ground.
Where the record is silent

A camera caught the pile, and the court watched the tape. It could not tell whether Popov would have held on had the crowd let him be. That question stays open. The court said so, and moved on.

The judgment, 2002

Judge Kevin McCarthy went back to the fox. A catch requires complete control, and Popov never proved one. But his attempt was cut short by the wrongdoing of a mob — so he held something the fox case never imagined: a pre-possessory interest. And Hayashi's pickup was clean. Neither claim could erase the other.

“An award of the ball to Mr. Popov… would be premised on the assumption that Mr. Popov would have caught the ball. That assumption is not supported by the facts.

— McCarthy, J., Popov v. Hayashi (2002)

“An equal and undivided interest in the ball.

— the court's declaration, Popov v. Hayashi (2002)
The auction, June 2003

The court ordered the ball sold and the money divided. At auction it brought

The sale price, before fees and taxes.

One half Alex Popov
One half Patrick Hayashi
Coda

The rule never changed.

Pursuit is not possession — on a beach in 1805, and in a ballpark walkway in 2002. What changed is what a court will do with the almost. The fox went home with the man who killed it, and the hunter got nothing. The baseball was sold, and the almost was worth exactly half.

Method

Quotations appear only from the two opinions. Where the record is silent — whether Pierson saw the chase; whether Popov would have completed the catch — the story marks the gap and does not resolve it. All facts of the incidents are drawn from the opinions and the secondary accounts listed above.

Limitations

  • The division shown is the court's split of the sale price before auction fees and taxes; the amounts each man ultimately kept were smaller.
  • The $450,000 auction figure (June 2003) is as widely reported at the time of sale, not a figure stated in either opinion.
  • The 1802 chase survives only through the case record and much later local accounts; details beyond the opinions are contested.
