Liebeck v. McDonald's A close reading of the trial evidence · 15 min read

Everyone knows the punchline — a woman spilled coffee, sued, and won millions. Almost none of what "everyone knows" survives contact with the trial record: the burns, the temperature policy, the 700 warnings, and the $800 offer.

The Gist In short
  • The injuries were catastrophic. Third-degree burns over roughly 6% of Stella Liebeck's body; eight days in hospital and skin grafts.
  • The temperature was a policy, not an accident. McDonald's held coffee at 180–190°F — hot enough to cause a third-degree burn in about 3 seconds.
  • The company had been warned. More than 700 prior burn complaints in a decade, yet the policy stood.
1
The myth

The story most people can still recite

Ask almost anyone about "the McDonald's coffee lawsuit" and a fully-formed narrative arrives, unbidden and confident: a woman spilled coffee on herself, sued a fast-food chain for her own clumsiness, and walked away with millions. It is invoked at dinner tables and in editorial pages as the definitive example of a litigious culture run amok — the case that "everyone knows," the shorthand for the frivolous lawsuit. It was parodied on Seinfeld. It has been cited in political speeches. It launched a thousand op-eds about tort reform.

What makes the case worth revisiting is not that the popular version is slightly off. It is that the popular version inverts almost every fact that mattered at trial. The woman was 79 years old. The coffee was not merely hot but held, by written corporate policy, at a temperature that scalded skin to the muscle in seconds. She did not go looking for millions; she asked for her medical bills. And the jury that everyone imagines as reckless or sentimental was, by the account of the trial record, responding to physical evidence — photographs, expert testimony, and the company's own documents.

This report reads the case back from the record. It relies on the trial evidence as summarized by the American Museum of Tort Law, the Cornell Legal Information Institute, contemporaneous news accounts, and the case brief archived by Quimbee — sources that quote the temperature specifications, the burn-timing testimony, the prior-complaint count, and the damages figures directly. Where the popular memory and the record diverge, the record wins.

2
What actually happened

Albuquerque, February 27, 1992

Stella Liebeck was 79 years old and had never sued anyone in her life. On the morning of February 27, 1992, she was a passenger in her grandson's Ford — a car without cup holders — at the drive-through of an Albuquerque McDonald's. After the car was parked so she could add cream and sugar, she steadied the cup between her knees and pulled off the lid. The cup tipped. The coffee poured into her lap, soaked into cotton sweatpants, and held against her skin.

The single detail the jokes always omit is that the sweatpants made it worse. Fabric traps scalding liquid against the body; it does not wick it away. What would have been a painful splash from cooler coffee became, at this temperature, a sustained burn. Liebeck received third-degree burns over roughly six percent of her body — her inner thighs, groin, buttocks, and perineum — with lesser burns over about sixteen percent. She was hospitalized for eight days, underwent skin grafting and debridement (the surgical removal of dead tissue), and by some accounts lost twenty percent of her body weight during a recovery that stretched across two years.

"One of the worst scald burns he had ever seen"

At trial, Liebeck's treating physician testified that hers was among the worst scald injuries he had encountered. The jury was shown graphic photographs of the burns — the skin burned away to the layers of muscle and fatty tissue beneath. This is the evidentiary center of the case that the punchline erases. The word "spilled" does a great deal of quiet work in the popular retelling, flattening a two-year surgical ordeal into an image of someone dabbing at a stain.

3
The temperature

180 to 190 degrees was written policy

The heart of the plaintiff's case was not that coffee is hot — everyone knows coffee is hot — but that McDonald's coffee was hotter than anyone had reason to expect, by design. During discovery, Liebeck's attorneys established that the company's operations manual required franchisees to hold coffee at 180 to 190 degrees Fahrenheit. Testing of coffee sold elsewhere in the city found it served at least twenty degrees cooler. Other establishments served coffee around 160 degrees; a home coffee maker produces coffee closer to 135–150 degrees.

Those twenty or thirty degrees are not a matter of taste. They are the difference between an injury and a catastrophe, and the trial made that difference quantitative. Expert testimony walked the jury through the physics of scalding: at 190 degrees, spilled coffee can produce a third-degree burn — the kind that requires skin grafting — in about three seconds. At 180 degrees it takes twelve to fifteen seconds. Lower the temperature to 160 degrees and the same burn takes about twenty seconds. Those extra seconds, the plaintiff argued, are exactly the time a person needs to pull scalding liquid off their skin before it destroys the tissue.

4
What the company knew

More than 700 people had been burned first

If the temperature had been an oversight, the case would look very different. It was not. During discovery, McDonald's produced records of more than 700 prior burn claims over roughly a decade — from 1982 to 1992 — some involving children, some involving third-degree burns like Liebeck's. The company had already settled a portion of those claims, reportedly paying out more than half a million dollars. It knew, in other words, that its coffee was injuring customers, and it had decided that the volume of injuries was tolerable against the billions of cups it served.

This is the fact that turned a product-liability claim into a punitive-damages case. Negligence is a failure to foresee harm; the 700 complaints established that McDonald's had foreseen it, priced it, and kept the policy. A quality-control witness reportedly testified that the company had no intention of lowering the temperature and did not regard the burns as significant. The trial judge, in declining to order a new trial, described the company's conduct as "callous," and characterized it in the damages ruling as "willful, wanton, and reckless."

5
The offer

She asked for $20,000. They offered $800.

The image of a greedy claimant chasing a deep pocket is perhaps the most durable part of the myth, and it is the one the record most cleanly demolishes. Before any lawsuit was filed, Liebeck contacted McDonald's, described her injuries, and asked the company to cover her medical costs — a figure of roughly ten to eleven thousand dollars — and to look at the temperature of its coffee so that others would not be harmed. Her total settlement request was about twenty thousand dollars. It was, in effect, a request to be made whole and a plea to prevent a repeat.

McDonald's responded with an offer of eight hundred dollars — a sum that did not cover even the hospital stay, let alone the grafts and the two years of care. Only after that offer, over a six-month back-and-forth, did Liebeck retain an attorney. The lawsuit was not the opening move of an opportunist; it was what remained after a badly injured 79-year-old asked to be reimbursed and was brushed off. The gulf between the two figures is the case's quiet moral center.

6
The verdict

The number that launched a thousand op-eds

The trial ran from August 8 to 17, 1994, before Judge Robert H. Scott in Bernalillo County. The jury found McDonald's 80 percent at fault and Liebeck 20 percent at fault for the spill. It awarded $200,000 in compensatory damages — for medical costs, pain, and suffering — which was reduced to $160,000 to reflect her share of responsibility. Then it added $2.7 million in punitive damages, and that is the figure that entered folklore.

What the folklore never mentions is what happened next. The trial judge reduced the punitive award to $480,000 — three times the compensatory damages — bringing the total to roughly $640,000. The parties then settled confidentially before an appeal was resolved, for an amount reported to be less than $500,000. Stella Liebeck did not receive $2.7 million, and she never received "millions." The headline number was a jury's signal, promptly trimmed by the court and then negotiated further down.

Why $2.7 million was not a random big number

The punitive figure looked absurd only if you did not know how the jury arrived at it. Punitive damages exist to deter, and deterrence is calibrated to the defendant's size — a fine that would ruin a corner café is a rounding error to a global chain. The plaintiff's team pointed the jury to McDonald's own coffee revenue, and the $2.7 million figure was reportedly set to approximate about two days of the company's coffee sales. It was a number designed to be noticed by a company that measured coffee in billions of cups, not a windfall for one burned customer.

7
The afterlife

How a scald became a slogan

Within days of the verdict, the case detached from its facts and began a second life as folklore. It arrived at a useful moment: an organized tort-reform movement was making the argument that American juries were out of control and that the civil justice system needed statutory caps and stricter rules. Liebeck's headline number — stripped of the burns, the temperature policy, the 700 complaints, and the judge's reduction — was the perfect exhibit. A 79-year-old burn victim became, in the retelling, a symbol of everything supposedly wrong with the right to sue.

The retelling worked because it was frictionless. "Woman spills coffee, wins millions" fits on a bumper sticker; "corporation ignores 700 burn reports, holds coffee at a temperature that scalds skin in three seconds, offers an injured retiree $800, and is trimmed to $640,000 by the judge" does not. The myth is not a lie so much as a compression — a lossy summary that happens to discard every fact that would change your verdict. Later documentary work, notably the 2011 film Hot Coffee, and retrospectives from outlets including The New York Times, have tried to restore the record, but the slogan long ago outran the correction.

The one change the myth never credits

There is a coda that the frivolous-lawsuit framing conveniently omits. According to news accounts documented at the time, the day after the verdict the Albuquerque McDonald's where Liebeck was burned was selling its coffee at about 158 degrees — a temperature at which a third-degree burn takes roughly sixty seconds rather than two to seven. The margin of safety had widened by an order of magnitude, and it widened as a direct consequence of the verdict everyone was busy mocking. Whatever else the case was, it was not costless theater; it changed the thing that had injured her.

8
What it means

Why the record still matters

The lasting value of reading Liebeck from the record is not that it exonerates a plaintiff who never needed exonerating. It is what the case reveals about how public memory of the legal system is made — and by whom. A verdict is a set of facts weighed by twelve people who saw the photographs and heard the testimony. A slogan is a verdict with the facts removed, engineered to travel. When the two compete, the slogan almost always wins, because it asks nothing of the listener.

That asymmetry has consequences beyond one case. The Liebeck myth helped normalize the belief that ordinary people who sue are mostly opportunists, and that belief shaped decades of argument about damage caps, comparative-fault rules, and access to courts. In several states, a plaintiff who was 20 percent at fault, as Liebeck was found to be, would recover less — or, under a strict contributory-negligence regime, nothing at all. The stakes of getting the story right, in other words, are not merely historical.

Read honestly, the hot-coffee case is not an argument that every large verdict is justified, nor that juries never err. It is a caution about confidence — about how easily a genuine, documented injury can be laundered into a punchline, and how much evidence has to be discarded to make the laundering work. The next time the case comes up as a shorthand for frivolity, the useful reply is not an opinion but a question: which of the facts, exactly, was frivolous — the burns, the 180 degrees, the 700 complaints, or the $800?
