# Voice & Tone Guide — Catterson
### Extracted from "The Naked Designer" (1,300-word cut) and its editing record

Use this to keep the portfolio copy in Catterson's real voice — not a generic
"designer portfolio" voice. Every rule below is pulled from an actual decision made
in the essay, with the reasoning that drove it. When in doubt, the organizing test is
his own: **"did this happen to me, or am I just saying it?"** If a line is just saying
it, cut or ground it.

---

## THE CORE POSTURE
Self-implication, not self-promotion. The essay only works because the person most
equipped to hide behind craft voluntarily removes it. Applied to a portfolio: state
what you did plainly and let the work testify. Don't sell. Don't perform confidence
you didn't earn. The credibility comes from being straight, including about the hard
parts and the things you got wrong (see the editorial piece: dropping the inflated
claims made it stronger). Confidence here is quiet and load-bearing, never salesy.

---

## HARD RULES (non-negotiable, carried from the essay)
- **No em dashes.** Ever. Use a period, a comma, or restructure. This is a house rule.
- **"Made," not "built."** "Built" reads as engineered. "Made" carries hand + judgment,
  which is the whole point. (His "think by making" ethos.)
- **"Artifact," not "work,"** for the made thing that testifies.
- **No subheads** in a personal essay. (In a portfolio, minimal structural labels are
  fine for scanning — but keep prose sections in flowing prose, not bulleted fragments.)
- Plain, direct language over decorated language.

---

## SENTENCE-LEVEL MOVES (what the prose actually does)

### 1. Cut hedges. They whisper "I wasn't in the room."
Real example: "surely" was cut from "The poem surely came the way everything comes"
because "surely is a hedge wearing a confident hat — it whispers 'I wasn't in the room'
when the sentence doesn't need the alibi." Kill: surely, mostly, somewhat, fairly,
a bit, I think, arguably, in a way, of course. If the grounding is already in the
sentence, the hedge is dead weight.

### 2. Unknot doubled logic. Prepositions that fold on themselves must go.
Real example: "rearranged something in me that I'm still living inside" → "rearranged
something I'm still living inside." The something can't be inside you AND be the thing
you live inside. When spatial or logical relations double back, cut until they resolve.

### 3. Fragments are deliberate dramatic beats. Use sparingly, on purpose.
Real examples: "Fell." / "A thread pulled." / "was I?" These land because they're rare
and earned. A fragment is a drumbeat, not a habit. One per section at most, at a moment
that deserves the stop.

### 4. Prefer the middle cut over the punchy one when the mood is a haunting.
Real example: given "It rearranged something I'm still living inside" vs. the aggressive
"It rearranged me." — he took the middle. The punchy version "trades the inhabitation
image for a punch, and this paragraph is a haunting, not a punch." Match the cut to the
emotional register. Velocity isn't always the goal; sometimes the image is.

### 5. Time markers: don't stack them. One per idea.
Real example: "I'm older now" was cut because the paragraph already marked time two
better ways (the tense shift itself, and "took me twenty years to understand"). If the
paragraph already tells you when, don't say it again.

### 6. Keep structural frames intact. Watch what a paragraph opens and closes on.
Real reasoning: a paragraph that "opens with 'I've never shaken' and closes with 'still
living inside'" has persistence at both ends — that frame is load-bearing, don't break
it for a shorter sentence. Edits should respect the architecture, not just the word count.

### 7. Specific words can be load-bearing. Know which ones you can't cut.
Real example: "still" is explicitly flagged as load-bearing and must not be cut ("does
anyone in it still mean something"). Before cutting a small word, check whether it's
carrying the weight of the claim. Some tiny words are the whole point.

### 8. Buried craft over explicit cleverness.
Real examples: the "never more" / Nevermore chime stays two words and subliminal, never
called out. The Lucid Dreaming self-recant is buried, no self-citation. The instinct:
let the sharp move work without pointing at it. Trust the reader to feel it.

---

## RHYTHM & TEXTURE
- Compression is already high. This is a writer who cuts muscle, not just fat. Short
  copy should feel dense and deliberate, every word paying rent.
- Vary sentence length deliberately. A run of medium sentences, then a short one that
  lands. The fragment or the short declarative is the payoff, so it needs the setup.
- Concrete over abstract. The essay grounds every abstract claim in a lived, specific
  image (five terminals, thirty minutes; the oxblood sweater unraveling). Portfolio copy
  should do the same: name the actual thing, the actual moment, not the category.
- Don't rescue yourself too quickly. The essay's power is staying in the discomfort
  longer than feels comfortable. Portfolio version: state the problem or the failure
  honestly before pivoting to the win. No premature "but here's what I learned."

---

## FOR THE PORTFOLIO SPECIFICALLY
- Screening-trigger scope means SHORT — but short in this voice = dense and true, not
  clipped and generic. Hook + proof, every word earned.
- Let the work carry the interest; keep words out of the playable/visual thing's way.
- Each piece's copy should pass the test: "did this happen to me, or am I just saying it?"
  If a sentence could appear on any designer's portfolio, it's not in his voice yet.
- Plain verbs, concrete nouns, no hedges, no em dashes, "made" not "built."
